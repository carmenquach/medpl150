Week Three: "Motherhood in Crisis" by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager, and Saidu Bah


The series of mini graphic novels, “Motherhood in Crisis”, explored the cruel realities of mothers living in Sierra Leone, a nation in West Africa. Each novel conveys the true stories of real women and their experience with motherhood, and this is done through an innovative visual format. The introduction starts off with some textual context, explaining how Sierra Leone’s healthcare systems are severely underfunded, and many patients end up traumatized as a result. Pregnant women often risk their lives just to survive and protect their child. The graphic novels share the four narratives of women, along with their hardships. After providing prior context, these stories are expressed through an interactive visual novel that truly showcases their experiences.

The artistic choice creates a more personal and urgent feeling for the readers, allowing us as readers to fully digest their story and empathize with their stories. For this reflection, I wanted to discuss the first story of the series. This is about a woman named Memunatu, who dealt with a very traumatic birthing of her baby. Because she lived in a rural province, there were no healthcare professionals to properly care for Memunatu. The only capable person of assisting in the childbirth was a local traditional herbalist, however she was incapable of considering Memunatu’s safety. She ended up in labor for two days, and unfortunately lost the child. On top of everything, she felt insecure and ashamed about her health issues, afraid to confide in anyone from her village.

In the end, she managed to head to the main city, Freetown, to receive proper medical care. There, she found solidarity among other women experiencing similar issues. Memunatu found confidence in herself overtime, thanks to her kind community and the accessibility to proper healthcare. This story is conveyed through a unique comic-like art style, utilizing textured and messy line art to portray the uncomfortable situation. Combining this with real-life photos of Memunatu, her husband, and the city clinic, this further enforces the story’s realism. The art of the novel is mostly muted and unsaturated, portraying the sense of tension and unease. Facial expressions are very detailed, showing the pain and misery that Memunatu experiences throughout the short visual novel. All of these artistic details allow us as readers to humanize the statistic of maternal women.

In the textual context mentioned earlier, the authors Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager, and Saidu Bah mention how “in the West African nation of Sierra Leone, one in 10 women die during childbirth. Healthcare systems are fragile – further strained by Ebola and now COVID-19 – and access to basic treatment is often limited”. The personal illustrations of the novel help readers empathize with Memunatu and many other women who deal with similar situations. We realize that this is more than a personal issue, rather a systemic issue of the nation itself. By visualizing the environment—the long paths to clinics and the sparse medical facilities—the artwork makes the structural failures of the healthcare system feel as real and personal as the physical pain of the women depicted.
